Apprentice years:

Inspira Mfg:

After completing the 20 week CNC operators course, I started my apprenticeship at Inspira Mfg. in Edmonton, Alberta where I spent the first 3 years of my apprenticeship. Right away I was introduced to and was using GibbsCAM for 4 axis milling. While at Inspira, I ran an Okuma turning center with Fanuc control and Mori Seiki turning center. I did some manual machining and surface grinding as well to fullfill my apprenticeship requirements, but the majority of the time I was running a Mazak 4 axis machining center similar to this Mazak AJV-32, using both Mazatrol conversational programming and G-code. This machine was great to learn on. It was old and had never been well maintained and always threw alarms, so it made me learn how to troubleshoot and really get into the weeds of the operators manual and perform preventative maintenance. It had a 40 tool magazine, Cat 50 taper and 2 pallets with a pallet changer.

The majority of parts we made at Inspira were for downhole oilfield. Sperry Sun / Haliburton was our biggest customer. Inspira was a great shop to learn in. I did a huge variety of parts, from one-offs to large and medium batch runs. The jobs and materials were diverse, and I learned how to do fast set-ups using multiple vices and WPC's. As apprentices, we were taught, the greatest effect on tool life was speed, feed and depth of cut in that order, and if you were trying to get faster, the best thing to do is increase depth of cut. I would argue, the best way to get faster is setting up multiple vices and using multiple WPC's when you can. Greatly reducing cycle times by reducing tool changes, rapid movements and just opening and closing the door.

At Inspira, we were an ISO 9001 shop. I learned how to hit tight tolerances and be accountable for my work. I learned about tracability, process control and quality control. I learned how to use boring heads, thread milling, how to make tooling when needed. I learned all about speeds and feeds and depths of cut, using HSS and carbide. I learned from mistakes and successes.

Looking back, Inspira was a really good fit for me because it allowed me the freedom to make decisions about how to go about my job, and trusted my instincts as they were developing. I was guided but not micro-managed.

Why I left Inspira Mfg:

By the time I was into my fourth year, Inspira was struggling as a company. They had a change in management, money was tight and they couldn't let me go to do my fourth year schooling in the next fall. At that time in Edmonton, with over 200 machine shops, the oilfields were booming and the joke amongst apprentices was "be careful where you step when you go out for lunch, you will likely fall into a better paying job". So like many others, I chased the buck and left Inspira after getting a pretty good offer that felt like a no-brainer. However, I was naive in thinking every shop would be pretty much like the one I was leaving. I found myself bouncing between shops trying to find my fit. Most shops already had a programmer and were looking for grunts and button pushers. I knew I wanted to program as well.

Armor Machine and Mfg:

I did a short stint of about 6 months at Armor machine. During my time at Armor, I gained some exposure to operating a 5 axis Heidenhain machine. The control system was quite different from what I was used to, and the programmers reliance on macros, variables, and counters made the workflow feel unconventional to me, as I was used to Mazatrol programming and G-code. The atmosphere there was not very supportive, and it seemed like some individuals were protective of their roles, which made learning and advancing more difficult.

After my training, I got stuck on a night shift which I was ok with. But I was basically just monitoring the machine, changing inserts and shoveling chips all night. A cut from one end to the other could take 45 minutes. Set-ups and programming was already done and I did not feel challenged or mentally stimulated. This shop was not a good fit for me, but I did find the parts and machines pretty cool. Some of the machines were huge, having upwards to 30' beds. The parts were called "whipstocks" for directional drilling. Another thing that I did find interesting was the way they used both left and right hand facemills for their roughing operations, and that way they were able to use an edge of the insert that would not have been utilized. Essentially doubling their tooling life. It was clever but situational. I was only ever able to duplicate that process one other instance later on.

Brunette job offer and moving to BC:

After the birth of our daughter in 2014, we were looking to move onto a small plot of land. At the same time, land prices around Edmonton and Leduc were skyrocketing, pretty much forcing us out of that market. I had always wanted to live in BC, even before going to Alberta, so we began searching the BC real estate market and found the Prince George area to be affordable land prices.

At the same time I was approached by a "head hunter" staffing agency who said there was a machine shop in Prince George looking for a CNC machinist. I arranged a meeting with the "then manager" of Brunette Machine Co. who told me his plans for expansion into CNC machines and invited me for a visit to see the PG shop.

I accepted a job offer and within a very short time, we had sold our 2 properties, sold our second car, and purchased a rural property near Prince George. I will say, I was totally misled by the man and the opportunity turned out to be "his vision" for the company, but not the owners vision. I was soon to meet the actual owner who informed me he never had any intentions to ever have CNC equipment in his business plan, and he didn't know why Tony hired me. With no consideration what-so-ever of how it had disrupted my families lives, I was laid off a week after Christmas, having been there less than 3 months.

Funny thing about that is, 10 or more years later and I hear they have some CNC equipment now. I guess he finally got with the times. Better late than never I guess, if he wants to stay competitive.

It is my understanding the guy who made all the promises and job offer was also let go not long afterwards. I would include though that, the short time I was there they really didn't have much work and I wasn't the only one laid off at the time. It really left me feeling deflated, having left a really good situation, and moving my family including a 10 month old baby.

Motion Canada:

When first hired at Motion, my primary role involved supporting and collaborating with Millwrights in the disassembly, inspection, repair, and reassembly of hydraulic cylinders and industrial components. I performed welding and manual machining of hydraulic cylinder parts—both repairs and new replacements—using manual lathes, mills, saws, drill presses, and broaching equipment, along with general shop tooling and presses.

As our shop was eventually given a small budget for new equipment, I used my CNC background to make the case for investing in a CNC machine. After the purchase of a Haas VF2 with thru spindle coolant and Renishaw probe, we brought on a second machinist, and my role shifted toward programming and operating the CNC, taking the lead on set-up, tooling and fixturing strategies, and production machining.

Later on, I began working closely with our lead field service technician and our hydraulic fluid specialist to design and implement new equipment for the mining industry. This included solid modeling, detailed drawings, and component design with deliberate consideration for the machining envelope and capabilities of our Haas VF2, for various mining applications, highlighting strong teamwork and cross department collaboration.

It was a role I genuinely enjoyed and one I likely would have remained in until retirement. Unfortunately, an environmental incident at the mine we supported eliminated much of our projected workload, and combined with corporate restructuring, our shop was ultimately closed in the spring of 2025.
